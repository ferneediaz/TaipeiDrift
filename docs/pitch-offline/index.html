The problem

Once GNSS is jammed, a low-cost drone drifts off course.

36 s

is all it takes to drift 50 m. Jamming is routine in Ukraine and around Taiwan's outlying islands.

Source: own measurement, inertial sensors alone, median of 30 flights of the Mid-Air dataset. Jamming: Breaking Defense, January 2026.

0:15 to 0:35. Read the picture from the left: GNSS is lost, and from that moment the path the drone really flies bends away from the course it believes it is on. With only its own inertial sensors, a low-cost drone is 50 m off after 36 seconds (median of 30 flights of the Mid-Air dataset, findings 2.4). Over our 4.3 km simulated flight the same sensors are 3 km off in the median. The drone cannot hold its route, find its target or come home. Fielded answers such as Vantor's Raptor need a GPU and licensed 3D map data. That does not fit a low-cost drone.

0:35 to 0:55. The drone already has a camera, an IMU, a barometer and a GNSS receiver that is now useless. We add three things. A sun sensor gives a heading that no magnet disturbs. A free aerial photo on a memory card is the map: every 300 m the camera picture is matched against it, and a check refuses fixes that do not fit. Over water, where the camera has nothing to match, four small antennas take bearings on ships' AIS radio. A laser range finder gives the height for the camera's ground speed: the demo flight uses it, the map-matching results on the next slides do not. One filter fuses it all and reports a position with its own uncertainty. Cost: from 875 dollars for the whole drone, taking the low end of every price range: a frontline FPV drone from 300 dollars plus our kit from 575 dollars. With the high end of every range it is 995 dollars. No licensed map data, and every part is sold over the counter. The sun sensor and the low-cost radio are designed and simulated, not built yet. Backup / our position

It is cheap, independent and honestly tested.

Price class

A $575 kit. The only other price we found: about $20,000 per aircraft.

Independence

Free maps and retail parts. Raptor needs a vendor licence per machine.

Rigour

Code frozen first. Sealed flights run once. Tests declared in advance.

Sources: DroneXL, May 2026 (Vermeer); Vantor developer guide; our cost slide; docs/simulation-results.md; docs/research/tuniu-level2-prereg.md.

The answer to: what is new here? The idea is proven and fielded, so we do not claim novelty or better accuracy. Three things are ours. Price class: the only published price we found for navigation by camera and map is Vermeer's, about 20,000 dollars per aircraft (DroneXL, 29 May 2026); Vantor, UAV Navigation and Delian publish none; our kit is 575 dollars at the lowest retail prices, on a drone that costs a few hundred. Ukraine's own Eagle Eyes is reported as cheap enough for kamikaze drones, but it is not for sale. Independence: Vantor's developer guide describes the Raptor licence as a file that grants the right to use the software on a specific machine and that can expire, and the product needs Vantor's 3D terrain data; ours runs on open aerial photos and parts sold over the counter, which fits Taiwan's rule of non-Chinese supply chains and avoids hanging on one foreign vendor. Rigour: the navigator was frozen at a tagged commit before the two sealed flights were run, once; the real-flight test on the Tuniu photos was written down and pushed before any test photo was processed; 304 automated tests pass. That is why the numbers can be believed, including the unflattering ones.

What a drone maker pays today to keep navigating under jamming. An imported anti-jam antenna (CRPA) costs 20,000 to 25,000 dollars on Ukraine's MICH 2000 long-range drone, which itself starts at 48,000 dollars (Defence Blog, 14 August 2026). Vermeer's visual positioning system, fielded in Ukraine, adds roughly 20,000 dollars per aircraft: up to four cameras, an Nvidia edge processor and a 3D terrain map (DroneXL, 29 May 2026). At the cheap end, a terminal-guidance module such as The Fourth Law's TFL-1 costs under 100 dollars, but it only steers the last 400 to 500 m and gives no position. Vantor, UAV Navigation and Delian publish no prices. Ours, at the lowest price of each range: 10 dollars of parts for the sun sensor, 575 dollars for everything we add, including a Raspberry Pi 5, a camera, a memory card, the range finder and the direction finder (575 to 595 across the ranges; see the cost slide). The comparison is hardware price only: those products are fielded and ours is a prototype.

Say it openly: the idea is proven. Tomahawk matched camera pictures against a map in the 1980s, and Ukraine's Eagle Eyes has done it in combat since 2023. Fielded systems are more accurate than we are today. Ours is the open, low-cost version with measured limits. Raptor Guide (Vantor, formerly Maxar; AIDC holds the Taiwan distribution rights and flight-tested it as AIxVNAV in August 2026): the maker states under 10 m RMSE, day and night; one published test of eight daytime flights measured 12.2 m mean; its developer guide requires a GPU and a per-device licence, and it relies on the vendor's licensed 3D terrain data; no public price. VNS01 (UAV Navigation): a 100 g, 5 W peripheral for the maker's own VECTOR autopilots; drift bounded to 30 m over unknown terrain; no public price. Vermeer VPS: up to four cameras, an Nvidia edge processor and a 3D terrain map; roughly 20,000 dollars per aircraft (DroneXL, 29 May 2026). Eagle Eyes: the software of Ukraine's special operations forces, with a map stitched from recent reconnaissance flights; no accuracy or price published; it is unrelated to Eagle Eyes Search, a search-and-rescue app for DJI drones. Also fielded: Osiris (Delian, Greece), 15 m CEP by day, under 300 g, 25 W, no public price. Ours: 12 to 32 m median on the sealed simulated flights; on real flights from other countries that an earlier version never saw, 60 to 131 m; over open water we use ships' radio, in simulation only. Target: a Raspberry Pi 5, $78, 50 g, 3 to 10 W. GPU-based systems: a Jetson kit, $399, 174 g, 7 to 25 W.

Sources: own timing on one laptop core, not yet on a drone computer; raspberrypi.com, CNX Software, NVIDIA, Jeff Geerling's board tests.

Measured with baseline/scripts/time_navigator.py on one core: 4.7 ms to read a frame and turn it north up, 6.0 ms of optical flow. One map fix: 68 ms for the smallest search (60 m radius), 274 ms at 150 m, 803 ms at 300 m, 2.0 s at the 600 m cap, with 5 zooms and 3 angles tried. The map is a grey image at 0.5 m per pixel, 4 MB per square kilometre. Not measured on drone hardware. An estimate only, if asked: a Raspberry Pi 5 core scores about 770 to 900 in Geekbench 6 single core against about 2,560 for an Apple M2, and our timing ran under Intel emulation at roughly 80 percent of native speed, so expect about two and a half times our numbers: about 30 ms per frame and about 5 s for the widest fix, still far inside the 30 s between fixes. Boards: Raspberry Pi 5 with 2 GB, 77.50 dollars (raspberrypi.com; the 4 GB model is 110), 50 g, 2.8 W idle and 9.7 W at full load; whether 2 GB is enough for the navigator has not been measured; Jetson Orin Nano Super developer kit, 399 dollars since July 2026 (249 at launch), 174 g, 7 to 25 W. Raptor's developer guide requires a GPU and ships a Jetson build; Vermeer uses an Nvidia edge processor. The live filter in the simulator and Ilhan's second matcher (which uses a learned matcher) are not included in these timings.

What is simulated

Demo and sealed flights. The demo autopilot flies on the true position.

Source: own tests, docs/simulation-results.md and docs/findings.md.

The expected questions. Night: an ordinary camera sees nothing; the known route is a thermal camera against the same map, as Raptor does; not started. AIS switched off or spoofed: over water only dead reckoning is left; in the demo flight the inertial sensors alone were 263 m off at the helipad. Fog: at 1 km of visibility the error grows up to three and a half times and the stated bound fails 2 to 25 percent of the time; where the ground was rebuilt since the map (UAV-VisLoc flight 01) the fixes do not help. Jammed from take-off: not covered, the system learns its camera scale while GNSS still works. Simulation: the demo and the sealed flights are simulated over real aerial imagery; the real-flight result is a replay of recorded photos; nothing was flown by us. In the live demo the filter's own error bound is too small (the truth is inside its 2 sigma only about half of the time), which is why the dashboard often shows EXCEEDED. The honest bound is the frozen navigator's on the recorded flights: held 100 percent.
